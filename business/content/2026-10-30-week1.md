**Days 1-7:** search Reddit for these phrases and answer 2-3 a day. Give a full answer with no link unless someone asks:
`job costing spreadsheet` · `how do I know if a job is profitable` · `contractor cash flow problems` · `track expenses per job`

**Answer template** (adapt it to each question, don't paste the same text everywhere):
> What fixed this for me: one cost log, with date / job # / category / amount, for every receipt and payroll run. Then a SUMIFS per job against the estimate. The two things people skip are equipment (rentals, dumpster) and overhead. Take your yearly overhead, divide by yearly revenue, and that % comes off every job's margin. A "30% job" is often 15% after that. Do it weekly, not at job end, or it's useless.

**Day 8 post (r/smallbusiness; read the sidebar rules first):**
**Title:** Contractors: a worked example of why your "best" job might barely break even (overhead math)
**Body:**
> Most small contractors judge jobs by gross margin: contract minus materials and labor. Take a kitchen remodel at $50k with $34k in costs. It looks like 32%.
>
> Now add overhead. Say insurance, the truck, software, phone and part-time office help come to $1,500/month, $18k a year, against $133k in revenue. That's about 13.5% of every dollar. The kitchen is really 19%, and a basement job that looked like 25% is really 12%.
>
> A spreadsheet setup that makes this visible:
> 1. One log of every cost: date, job #, category (labor/materials/subs/equipment/other), amount
> 2. SUMIFS per job, compared to the estimate (% of budget used)
> 3. Overhead allocated by each job's share of revenue
> 4. A month-by-month cash view, because profitable and broke are different problems
>
> I built a spreadsheet that does all four (free version for 3 jobs). Happy to share it if it's useful.
